Each problem passed the assistant's tests because every upstream in them answered at once, with a reply. None was slower than another, none failed while another worked, and none ignored its caller, so nothing was ever left waiting, and no test looked for what was.

A goroutine that can't finish is a leak, and Go's blog on pipelines says why it matters: "Goroutines are not garbage collected; they must exit on their own." A send on a channel with no buffer waits for a receive, so once `Fanout` stopped receiving, each slower upstream's goroutine waited forever, "and heap references in goroutine stacks keep data from being garbage collected." With a buffer, "send operations complete immediately if there's room in the buffer", and a buffer the size of the list of upstreams always has room.

A context made for a call is only useful if the call gets it. `context.WithCancel` gives a context whose `Done` "is closed when the returned cancel function is called", and its docs say why to call it: "Canceling this context releases resources associated with it". The assistant made `asking`, deferred its cancel, and then passed the caller's `ctx` to the upstreams, so the cancel stopped nothing. Asking every upstream already costs a reply from each; not canceling the ones that lose adds every token they go on to write.

Waiting is where a caller's deadline is kept. A receive on its own waits for as long as the slowest upstream takes. In a `select` with `ctx.Done()`, the caller's deadline ends the wait, and with the buffer, the goroutines still out finish when their upstreams do.
