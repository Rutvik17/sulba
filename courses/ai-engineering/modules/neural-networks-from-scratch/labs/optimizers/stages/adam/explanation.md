Started at 0, the average of a steady gradient `$g$` after `$t$` steps is `$(1 - \beta_1^t) g$`, short of `$g$` by the share of its weight still on the 0 it started from, and the same goes for `$v$` with `$\beta_2$`. Dividing by `$1 - \beta^t$` gives back `$g$` and `$g^2$`, and the step comes out at the learning rate from the first step on, as your line on the chart does.

Without the correction, the two averages start short by different amounts. With PyTorch's defaults, `$\beta_1$` = {% $fig.beta1 %} and `$\beta_2$` = {% $fig.beta2 %}, `$v$` starts far further from its value than `$m$` does, so `$m / \sqrt{v}$` comes out too large: {% $fig.first %} learning rates at the first step, and {% $fig.peak %} at step {% $fig.peak_step %}, before it settles. The closer `$\beta_2$` is to 1, the larger and longer the overshoot, as the chart's slider shows.

Since `$\hat{m} / \sqrt{\hat{v}}$` doesn't change when every gradient is multiplied by the same number, Adam's step is set by the learning rate, not by the gradients' size, which is why its learning rates are so much smaller than SGD's. The weight decay added to the gradient goes through the same ratio, and with no gradient it moves every weight by the learning rate whatever its size. AdamW, in the next stage, takes it out.
