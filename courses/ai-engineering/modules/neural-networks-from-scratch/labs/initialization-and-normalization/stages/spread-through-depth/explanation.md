A layer's output `$y = W x$` adds up `$n$` products, so its mean square is `$n$` times the weights' mean square times the inputs'. A weight drawn from `$U(-a, a)$` has a mean square of `$a^2 / 3$`, and a ReLU keeps half of a symmetric signal's mean square. With Kaiming's `$a = \sqrt{2} \sqrt{3 / n}$`, the three cancel, `$n \cdot \frac{2 \cdot 3 / n}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1$`, and on average the signal keeps its size however deep the network. That's the gain: `$\sqrt{2}$` makes up for the half a ReLU takes away.

`nn.Linear`'s own start is Kaiming's with `$a = \sqrt{5}$`, a gain of `$\sqrt{1/3}$`, which comes to `$U(-1/\sqrt{n}, 1/\sqrt{n})$`. Before a ReLU, each such layer keeps a sixth of the mean square, {% $fig.default %} of the size, and {% $fig.hidden %} layers keep {% $fig.after %} of it. Xavier's start, with a gain of 1, keeps half the mean square through a ReLU in a layer as wide as its input, {% $fig.xavier %} of the size: its gain is for layers with no nonlinearity, or with one that keeps the signal's size, and calling `calculate_gain("relu")` for it gives the ReLU's.

The chart multiplies Kaiming's range by `$c$`. Each layer then multiplies the size by about `$c$`, so after {% $fig.layers %} layers a start {% $fig.off %} too small leaves {% $fig.small %} of the signal's size, and one {% $fig.off %} too large multiplies it by {% $fig.large %}. Depth turns a small error in the start into a large one, which is why the gain matters. Each layer's own weights, drawn at random, also move the signal a little up or down, so the chart's line wanders around `$c$` to the power of the layer.
